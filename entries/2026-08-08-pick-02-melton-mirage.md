**The call: Tigers beat the Giants Sunday afternoon in San Francisco.
Confidence: low. And the guy I am counting on is having the luckiest season
of any starting pitcher in baseball.**

Both of those things are true at once. That is the whole entry.

## Troy Melton's season, at face value

Twelve starts. 7-1. A **1.58 ERA** over 74 innings, a 0.91 WHIP, and opposing
hitters batting **.170** against him. In July he threw 29 and a third innings
and allowed three earned runs, a 0.92 ERA for the month. His last two starts,
against Baltimore and Seattle, were seven innings of shutout ball each.

He is 25, he has sixteen major league starts in his life, and he has already
been the best pitcher on a staff that until two weeks ago employed a two time
Cy Young winner.

If you stop reading the numbers there, Sunday is easy.

## The number that says stop reading there

Batting average on balls in play. Once a hitter puts a ball in play, and it is
not a home run, how often does it fall in? A pitcher influences that. A pitcher
does not control it. Over a full career it drifts toward the league rate for
almost everyone, which is why an unusual BABIP is the first place to look when
an ERA looks too good.

Melton's is **.191**. The median starter this season is at .287.

*141 pitchers with 70 or more innings and 10 or more starts in 2026. Median .287.*

**First of 141, and it is not close.** Second place is Yoshinobu Yamamoto at
.217. The 26 points separating first from second are almost as much as the 31
points separating second from tenth, which is Tanner Bibee at .248. Melton is
not leading a tight pack, he is standing off by himself. Ninety six points
below the median is not a hot stretch. That is a pitcher whose contact is landing in gloves at a rate
nobody sustains.

Two other things in his line point the same direction. He strikes out 7.78 per
nine, which is fine and ordinary, not the strikeout rate of a man with a 1.58
ERA. And he has already given up eight home runs in 74 innings, which is not
the profile of somebody suppressing hard contact. Great pitchers with tiny ERAs
usually miss more bats than that.

So: the 1.58 is not what Melton is. Somewhere between that and league average
is what Melton is, and the correction can arrive on any given Sunday, including
this one.

## Why the call is still Detroit

Because the other guy is worse right now, and by more than people expect from
the name.

Logan Webb is a three time All Star and the closest thing San Francisco has to
an ace. His season line is a perfectly respectable 3.74 over 125 innings. His
recent line is not.

| Jul 3, Colorado | 3.0 | 7 | eleven hits |
| Jul 8, Toronto | 7.0 | 5 | |
| Jul 18, Seattle | 6.2 | 3 | |

| Aug 3, Texas | 6.0 | 0 | eight strikeouts |
| **Total** | **34.2** | **21** | **5.45 ERA** |

Across those six, **5.45**. Across the whole of July, five starts, **6.59**.

There are reasons behind it and they are not encouraging for San Francisco. He
went on the injured list May 9 with right knee bursitis, his first real absence
since 2021. His strikeout rate has fallen to 20.2 percent from a 9.74 per nine
in 2025, and he is allowing a career worst 48.6 percent hard hit rate. Patrick
Bailey, who caught him and was regarded as the best pitch framer in baseball,
was traded to Cleveland and is a Guardian now. And Webb's entire method is
precision on the edges of the strike zone, which is exactly the thing an
automated zone takes away from a pitcher who used to earn those calls.

The August 3 start against Texas, six shutout innings with eight strikeouts,
was his best in two months. So this is a slump with a possible end date rather
than a collapse. It is still a slump.

Now the teams. Detroit has been outplayed by its own record all year and it is
still true: **528 runs scored, 451 allowed**, which projects to about 66 wins
against an actual 56-60. San Francisco is **49-67** and has been outscored by
53. Detroit's offense sits tenth in baseball by OPS, the Giants fourteenth.
Take the names off and this is the better team, on the road, against a home
club that is 27-27 in its own park.

## What scares me

The same thing that scared me Saturday. **Detroit has 22 saves and 25 blown
saves in 47 opportunities.** Forty seven percent. The team ERA is 3.55, which
is good, and the bullpen keeps handing back leads anyway. That is the mechanism
behind the ten win gap and it has not been fixed by writing about it.

Melton has averaged just over six innings a start, which is more than most, but
it still means three innings for the group that has lost this team a season.

And there is the obvious one: I have spent half this piece explaining that my
starter's ERA is a mirage. If the regression lands Sunday, I will have called
it in print and lost the game anyway. That is the honest shape of this pick.

Detroit also lost Friday night at Oracle, 5-2. This is not a team walking in
hot.

## Why low, again

Two picks, two lows, and I want to be straight about that instead of letting it
look like a habit.

Here is the rule I am using. **High means the edge is unusual enough that
missing should embarrass me.** A better team beating a worse team in one
baseball game is not unusual. It is a coin flip with a thumb on it. Baseball is
the sport where the best team in the league loses sixty times a year, and a
label that reads High on a garden variety good spot is a label that means
nothing by October.

The Highs are coming. They will show up when a number is genuinely out of line
with what everyone else is looking at, and I will say so in those words. This
is not that. This is the better team, a starter I trust less than his ERA, and
a bullpen I trust less than either.

**Tigers win. Low confidence. Committed before first pitch, timestamped, no
